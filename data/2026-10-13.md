Abstract: Translating visual brain activity into natural language offers a powerful window into human cognition and neural semantic representation. Most prior brain-captioning approaches project fMRI signals into language space using paired supervision. However, the widely used Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) was built based only on image-fMRI pairs, while existing methods rely on MS-COCO captions as indirect textual supervision. Critically, during fMRI acquisition, participants observe only cropped image regions, whereas the associated MS-COCO captions describe full scenes. This mismatch creates a fundamental gap between neural representations and textual supervision, introducing an inherent semantic loss in NSD-based brain captioning. To mitigate this limitation, we reformulate brain captioning as Neural-Grounded Visual Captioning, where language is generated directly from visually grounded neural evidence rather than text supervision. We propose MindVLM, a framework that maps fMRI signals into subject-aware visual tokens and leverages a frozen vision language model (VLM) to produce interpretable and personalized descriptions. Instead of point-wise regression, MindVLM focuses on extracting semantically relevant visual cues from neural activity, naturally accommodating partial observability and inter-subject variability. Extensive experiments on NSD show that MindVLM consistently improves caption quality across multiple VLM backbones, including LLaVA-v1.5, Qwen2.5-VL, and Gemma3, beyond intrinsic model priors. Despite requiring neither text supervision nor an LLM during training and operating under an image-grounded inference paradigm distinct from conventional captioning methods, MindVLM remains competitive with supervised approaches, suggesting that subject-specific neural grounding provides an efficient, scalable, and interpretable path toward faithful brain-to-language generation.

Abstract: Hardware malware detectors (HMDs) classify programs as benign or malicious based on hardware performance counter (HPC) data. Previous studies have shown that ensemble classifiers outperform other classifiers on detection metrics. However, the area, power, and timing costs of HMDs remain open questions. We address these questions across a comprehensive design space comprising multiple classifier families, variable HPC sets, and ensemble learning methods. We designed a pipeline to reconstruct selected trained models, generate synthesizable register-transfer-level (RTL) implementations, validate each design against a bit-accurate golden model, and evaluate the implementations. The results show that tree- and rule-based detectors achieve the highest detection performance with nearly an order of magnitude less area and several times lower energy than function-based classifiers. Reducing the number of HPC inputs to fit a processor's concurrent-counter limit preserves detection performance while substantially reducing area, power, and delay.
